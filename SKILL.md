# Human Voice

This skill exists because AI-generated text has recognizable tics. Readers spot them instantly, and on platforms like LinkedIn they destroy credibility — the whole point of posting is to sound like a real person with real experience. Every rule below removes one of those tics.

The core principle: **people say what happened, plainly.** They don't build drama around a simple statement. They don't manufacture symmetry. They write the way they'd talk to a colleague over coffee.

Why AI writes badly: LLMs regress to the statistical mean. Specific, unusual facts (statistically rare) get replaced by generic, positive statements that could apply to any topic (statistically common). The subject becomes less specific and more exaggerated at the same time. Every rule here pushes the opposite way: toward the concrete, the uneven, the personal.

Sources merged into this skill: original human-voice rules; Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" (WikiProject AI Cleanup, CC BY-SA 4.0); concepts from Hardik Pandya's "stop-slop" (MIT).

## PART A — BANNED PATTERNS

### A1. Negation-before-the-point (the worst offender)

AI states what something is NOT before saying what it IS. Humans never do this unless they're genuinely correcting a misconception someone actually holds.

**Banned:**
- "It's not about speed. It's not about tools. It's about mindset."
- "I didn't eat pizza. I didn't eat a burger. I ate strudel."
- "This isn't just a tool — it's a way of thinking."
- "Not only dismissive, but also unnecessarily harsh."
- Reversed form: piling up "X rather than Y" constructions.

**Instead:** Just say the thing. "I ate strudel." Only negate when a real, specific misconception exists AND you name it once: "Most people think outcome-based billing means fixed price. It doesn't — here's the difference."

### A2. Staccato dramatization

Short sentence. Shorter sentence. One word. — This rhythm screams AI. Humans have uneven rhythm: some sentences run long and wander a bit, others are short, and the mix is irregular.

**Banned:**
- "We shipped it. On time. Under budget. No excuses."
- One-word paragraphs used for effect ("Wrong.", "Exactly.", "Read that again.")

**Instead:** "We shipped it on time and under budget, which honestly surprised even us."

### A3. Rule of three

AI defaults to triplets: "fast, simple, and effective", three adjectives, three parallel clauses, three bullet groups — used to make thin analysis look comprehensive. One triplet in a whole text can pass; a pattern of them cannot. Use one item or two, or four if the list genuinely has four things. Two items beat three.

### A4. Rhetorical question → self-answer

**Banned:** "So what does this mean? It means that..." / "Why does this matter? Because..." / "The result? A 40% increase."

**Instead:** Fold the answer into a statement. "This matters because..."

### A5. Stock openers, transitions, and meta-announcements

Banned phrases:
- "Here's the thing:"
- "Let me explain."
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "Let's dive in" / "deep dive"
- "Picture this:"
- "Spoiler alert:", "Hot take:", "Unpopular opinion:" (unless genuinely unpopular)
- Meta-announcements of what the text will do: "In this post I'll cover...", "The rest of this essay explores...", "Below is a breakdown of..."
- Any "here's what/this/that" throat-clearing before the actual point.

Start in the middle of the story instead: "A client told me yesterday that..." / "Three weeks into the project, we realized..."

### A6. Buzzword inflation and AI vocabulary

Banned: game changer, unlock potential, take it to the next level, seamless, powerful, revolutionary, cutting-edge, leverage (as a verb for "use"), elevate, empower, supercharge, harness, delve, robust, landscape ("the AI landscape"), journey ("my journey"), navigate ("navigating uncertainty"), foster, streamline, pivotal, crucial, testament, tapestry, underscore, showcase, boasts, garner, intricate, meticulous, vibrant, enduring, bolstered, interplay, "align with", "valuable insights".


These specific words are statistically overused by LLMs and co-occur: where there is one, there are likely others. Their synonyms are fine — the tell is these exact words.

Replace with the concrete thing: not "this unlocks massive potential" but "this cut our onboarding from two weeks to three days."

### A7. Dressed-up verbs instead of "is" and "has"

AI avoids plain copulas. **Banned as replacements for is/has:** serves as, stands as, marks, represents, boasts, features, maintains, offers, refers to, "ventured into X as a", "began his career as" (when "was" works).

"The gallery is LAAA's exhibition space" — not "serves as LAAA's exhibition space." Plain verbs generally: wrote (not authored), used (not utilized), moved (not relocated), tried (not attempted), died (not passed away).

### A8. Superficial "-ing" analysis endings

AI fakes depth by attaching a present-participle phrase to the end of sentences: "...highlighting the collaborative nature of the work", "...reflecting its continued relevance", "...contributing to the broader development of the field", "...ensuring long-term success."

These read as analysis but say nothing. Cut the phrase or replace it with a real, specific consequence.

### A9. Puffed-up significance

**Banned:** claims that arbitrary aspects of a topic "represent a shift", "mark a pivotal moment", "set the stage for", "leave an indelible mark", "reflect broader trends", or situate the subject amid manufactured "debates" and "discussions" it supposedly "raises questions about."

If something matters, show the specific way it mattered to a specific person or outcome.

### A10. Weasel attributions and lazy extremes

**Banned:** "experts argue", "observers have noted", "some critics say", "industry reports suggest", "is widely regarded as" — vague authorities carrying opinions.

Also banned: "every", "always", "never", "everyone knows" doing vague work ("it always comes down to trust"). Name who, name when, or drop the claim.

### A11. Vague declaratives

**Banned:** sentences that announce something exists instead of saying what it is: "The implications are significant." / "The reasons are structural." / "The results speak for themselves."

Name the specific implication, the specific reason, the specific result.

### A12. False agency and passive voice

**Banned:** inanimate things performing human actions: "the complaint becomes a fix", "the decision emerged", "the data tells a story", "the strategy writes itself."

**Banned:** passive constructions hiding the actor: "mistakes were made", "it was decided that."

Every sentence needs a human subject doing something. "We misjudged the timeline" — not "the timeline was misjudged."

### A13. Fake symmetry and over-formatting

- Paragraphs of identical length, each following the same internal structure
- Every bullet starting with a **Bolded Phrase:** + colon (the signature AI list format)
- Emoji as bullets or in headings (🚀 ✅ 💡 📌)
- Em dashes overused — in every other sentence, doing the job of commas, parentheses, or colons — mimicking punched-up sales copy
- Title Case In Headings where sentence case is natural
- Excessive boldface, "key takeaways" style
- Tiny tables for information that reads naturally as a sentence
- Hashtags (never, unless the user explicitly asks)
- A tidy zoom-out ending: "In conclusion...", "In summary...", "At the end of the day, it all comes down to..."

Humans write paragraphs of uneven length. Some points get three sentences, some get ten words.

### A14. Commanding the reader

**Banned:** "Remember this.", "Read that again.", "Let that sink in.", "Save this post."

A strong closing line challenges the reader through content or an honest question — never through a command about how to read.

### A15. False modesty, engagement bait, hand-holding

- "I never expected this post to blow up, but..."
- "Agree? 👇" / "Thoughts?"
- "Comment 'AI' and I'll send you the guide"
- Didactic disclaimers: "It's important to note that...", "It's worth remembering..."
- Softening and justifying instead of stating: trust the reader's intelligence.

A genuine question at the end is fine if the author actually wants answers: "How are you handling this with your own team?" One question, specific, no emoji arrow.

### A16. Hedging filler and empty adverbs

Cut: "arguably", "in many ways", "it's worth noting that", "at its core", "fundamentally", "essentially", "in essence", "needless to say".

Cut adverbs that add no information: "incredibly", "truly", "deeply", "absolutely", "genuinely" (when decorative). If a sentence survives without the word, the word was filler.

### A17. Narrator-from-a-distance

**Banned:** observing people from orbit: "People often struggle with delegation." / "Many founders face this dilemma." / "Nobody designed this system."

**Instead:** put the reader in the room: "You've probably caught yourself redoing a task you already delegated." Specifics beat abstractions; "you" beats "people."

## PART B — WHAT HUMAN WRITING DOES INSTEAD

- **Concrete over abstract.** "A client told me last Tuesday" beats "many clients face". Numbers, moments, named situations.
- **Starts in medias res.** No warm-up paragraph. First sentence drops the reader into the situation.
- **Uneven rhythm.** Mix long, winding sentences with short ones — irregularly, not as a device. Never three consecutive sentences of matching length.
- **First-person experience.** "I noticed", "we got burned by", "it took us three tries". Opinion stated as opinion.
- **Allows imperfection.** A digression, an admission of uncertainty ("I'm still not sure this scales"), a sentence that trails into a side thought. Occasional definitive claims too — "this was the best decision we made" — AI avoids committing; humans commit.
- **Repeats words naturally.** Humans reuse the same word; AI cycles synonyms mechanically ("elegant variation"). If the word is right, use it again.
- **Simple is/has phrases.** "There is a", "it has a" — empirically more common in human writing.
- **One idea per piece.** A post makes one point well instead of three points weakly.
- **Ends with substance.** The last line is either the sharpest version of the point, or an honest question. Never a summary, never a command. (Note: a strong closing line is a deliberate LinkedIn-voice choice, kept intentionally — don't flatten endings into nothing.)

## PART C — SELF-CHECK BEFORE DELIVERING

Read the draft and check:

1. Any "it's not X, it's Y" constructions? → rewrite as direct statement
2. More than one triplet? → break the pattern
3. Any banned phrase from A5, A6, A15, A16? → cut or replace with something concrete
4. Any "serves as / marks / boasts / features" standing in for is/has? → restore the plain verb
5. Sentence endings with decorative "-ing" analysis? → cut or make specific
6. Passive voice or an inanimate thing doing a human verb? → name the person, make them the subject
7. Vague declarative ("The implications are significant")? → name the specific thing
8. "Every/always/never/everyone" doing vague work? → name who and when, or drop it
9. Narrator-from-a-distance ("People often...")? → put the reader in the scene
10. Meta-announcement of the text's structure? → delete, let the text move
11. Do three consecutive paragraphs share length and structure? → vary them
12. Rhetorical question answered by the author? → fold into a statement
13. Bolded-header bullets, emoji bullets, Title Case headings, em-dash pile-up? → reformat as prose
14. Does it end with a summary or a command? → replace with the sharpest form of the point
15. Read it aloud (mentally): would a person say this sentence to a colleague? If it sounds like a keynote speech, rewrite it as conversation.

Scoring (from stop-slop): rate the draft 1–10 on directness (statements, not announcements), rhythm (varied, not metronomic), trust (respects reader intelligence), authenticity (sounds human), density (nothing cuttable). Below 35/50 → revise.

## PART D — EXAMPLE TRANSFORMATION

**Before (AI voice):**
> It's not about the tools. It's not about the process. It's about the people.
>
> In today's fast-paced world, teams that invest in themselves win. Period.
>
> Here's the thing: growth is a choice. A powerful one. A game-changing one.
>
> Remember this. 🚀

**After (human voice):**
> Last month one of our developers spent a weekend learning a framework nobody asked him to learn. Two weeks later it saved a client project that was quietly going sideways.
>
> Nobody planned that. But it's what happens on teams where people invest in themselves — the payoff shows up in places you couldn't have scheduled.
>
> The cheapest thing a company can buy is a curious engineer's spare time. Most companies still don't.

## PART E — DETECTION REFERENCE: SIGNS OF AI WRITING (full guide)

Use this part when reviewing or auditing text for AI tells, not when writing. Adapted in full from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" (WikiProject AI Cleanup, CC BY-SA 4.0); Wikipedia-only procedural material (wiki markup, citation templates, draft review) omitted.

#### Caveats before using this guide

- **These are signs, not proof.** LLMs are trained on human writing, so every pattern here also appears in human text — editorials, blogs, fan fiction. What matters is density and combination, not a single hit.
- **The list is descriptive, not prescriptive.** It catalogs observations from thousands of real AI-generated texts.
- **Don't trust AI detection tools.** GPTZero and similar perform better than chance but have non-trivial error rates, and are easily thrown off by paraphrasing, formatting changes, or models they weren't trained on.
- **Don't trust your gut either.** Studies show average humans distinguish AI text from human text at roughly chance level. Heavy LLM users reach about 90% accuracy — meaning even experts make ~1 false accusation per 10 calls.
- **The lines keep blurring.** Human speech and writing have been measurably drifting toward LLM patterns since 2024 (word choices, semantics, even podcasts). Writers also deliberately avoid "AI tropes" to dodge accusations.

#### Why AI writes this way

LLMs are statistical machines: they infer the most likely next text based on a huge training corpus. The output **regresses to the mean** — toward what's statistically common and broadly applicable.

Consequence: specific, unusual, nuanced facts (statistically rare) get replaced by generic, positive descriptions (statistically common). The highly specific *"inventor of the first train-coupling device"* becomes *"a revolutionary titan of industry."* The original's analogy: it's like shouting louder and louder that a portrait shows a uniquely important person, while the portrait itself fades from a sharp photograph into a blurry, generic sketch. The subject becomes **less specific and more exaggerated at the same time**.

This smoothing-over is simultaneously the technology's strength and its most reliable tell.

### PART 1: CONTENT PATTERNS

#### 1.1 Undue emphasis on significance, legacy, and broader trends

**Words to watch:** *stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, a vital/significant/crucial/pivotal/key role/moment, underscores/highlights its importance/significance, reflects broader, symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting..., contributing to the..., setting the stage for, marking/shaping the..., represents/marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted*

AI puffs up the importance of its subject by claiming that arbitrary aspects of it "represent," "reflect," or "contribute to" some broader theme. There is a distinct, identifiable repertoire of phrasings for this.

Typical output: an institute's founding "marked a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional statistics" and "represented a significant shift toward regional statistical independence... part of a broader movement." A town's roads "helped solidify its role as a regional hub."

Two sub-patterns:

- **Manufactured debates.** AI situates subjects amid broader "discussions" they supposedly provoke: "has generated debate about authenticity, consent, and the psychological effects...", "prompted broader reflection on mortality and memory in a digital age", "raising philosophical questions about identity."
- **Importance despite mundanity.** AI adds significance statements even to etymology or population data — sometimes with a hedging preamble admitting the subject is minor, before talking about its importance anyway: "Though it saw only limited application, it contributes to the broader history of early aviation engineering."

In biology topics, AI over-emphasizes connections to the broader ecosystem and belabors conservation status even when the status is unknown and no efforts exist: "Currently, there is no specific conservation assessment... However, the general health of the ecosystem is crucial for the survival of this and other endemic species."

#### 1.2 Canned emphasis on notability, attribution, and media coverage

**Words to watch:** *independent coverage, local/regional/national media outlets, music/business/tech outlets, trade publications, profiled in, written by a leading expert, active social media presence*

AI proves importance by hitting readers over the head with coverage claims — listing where a subject appeared and specifying what kind of sources those are: "featured in *Wired*, *Refinery29*, and other prominent media outlets", "profiled in multiple high-quality, independent, and widely-read outlets." It often attributes its own superficial analysis to those sources. More common in 2025+ output.

A specific idiosyncrasy: noting that a person or business "maintains an active social media presence" / "a strong digital presence, particularly on Instagram." This wording was rare before ~2024.

#### 1.3 Superficial analyses

**Words to watch:** *highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing..., ensuring..., reflecting/symbolizing..., contributing to..., cultivating/fostering..., encompassing..., enhancing..., valuable insights, align/resonate with*

AI inserts shallow interpretation of facts, typically by attaching a **present-participle ("-ing") phrase to the end of a sentence**: "...creating a lively community within its borders", "...further enhancing its significance as a dynamic hub", "...reflecting its continued relevance in the regional transportation landscape", "...underscoring the enduring practice of pious patronage."

These read as analysis but say nothing. Newer chatbots with web search attach them to named sources ("Roger Ebert highlighted the lasting influence...") regardless of whether the source says anything close.

#### 1.4 Promotional and advertisement-like language

**Words to watch:** *boasts a, vibrant, rich, profound, enhancing, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, renowned, featuring, diverse array*

LLMs have serious trouble keeping a neutral tone. Even prompted for neutrality, output drifts toward advertising or travel-guide prose: "Nestled within the breathtaking region of Gonder, X stands as a vibrant town with a rich cultural heritage... offers visitors a fascinating glimpse into the diverse tapestry of Ethiopia."

- **Cultural-heritage reverence:** anything framable as heritage (even an electronics industry) gets constant reminders of its importance.
- **Press-release tone for people/companies:** "These projects align with the company's goals of reducing its environmental footprint, improving operational efficiency, and fostering community development." (Note that example also stacks a rule-of-three.)
- Older models (GPT-4 era) were blatantly positive; newer ones are **subtly** positive and avoid obvious superlatives like "the best" — which makes the *specific vocabulary* more diagnostic than overall enthusiasm.

#### 1.5 Vague attributions and overgeneralization (weasel wording)

**Words to watch:** *industry reports, observers have cited, experts argue, some critics argue, several sources/publications (when few are cited), such as (before exhaustive lists), is described in scholarship as, modern researchers treat...*

AI attributes opinions to unnamed authorities: "is of interest to researchers and conservationists", "efforts are ongoing to monitor..." It also **exaggerates the quantity of sources** — presenting one or two sources' views as widely held, citing "reviewers" (plural) while quoting one person, or implying a list of examples is non-exhaustive when nothing suggests more exist: "was widely interpreted as...", "publications such as X and Y have presented..."

#### 1.6 Outline-like conclusions about challenges and future prospects

**Words to watch:** *Despite its... faces several challenges..., Despite these challenges..., Challenges and Legacy, Future Outlook*

A rigid formula, usually at the end of a rigidly-outlined text:

1. "Despite its [positive words], [subject] faces challenges, including..."
2. → ends with either a vaguely positive reassurance or speculation about how "ongoing initiatives" and "future investments" could help.

Real examples: "Despite its industrial and residential prosperity, Korattur faces challenges typical of urban areas, including... With its strategic location and ongoing initiatives, Korattur continues to thrive." / "Despite these challenges, the versatility of pyroelectric materials positions them as critical components for sustainable energy solutions."

The tell is the **formula**, not any mention of difficulties.

#### 1.7 Definitional openers for list-like or broad titles

AI introduces a title as if it were a standalone entity to define: "Catchment area (health) **refers to** the geographic area from which...", "The 'List of songs about Mexico' **is a curated compilation of** musical works that..." Humans introduce topics; AI defines labels.

### PART 2: LANGUAGE AND GRAMMAR

Linguistic patterns occur consistently regardless of subject matter, which gives AI text an identifiable "voice."

#### 2.1 High density of "AI vocabulary" words

**Words to watch:** *additionally (sentence-initial), align with, boasts (= "has"), bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate/intricacies, key (adjective), landscape (abstract noun), meticulous/meticulously, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry (abstract noun), testament, underscore (verb), valuable, vibrant*

Multiple studies confirm LLMs statistically overuse specific words; these words surged in text produced after 2022 and **co-occur**: where there is one, there are likely others. One or two in a text may be coincidence; many of them, many times, is one of the strongest tells.

The set shifts by model era:

- **2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4 era):** additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial, **delve**, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate, interplay, key, landscape, meticulous, pivotal, underscore, **tapestry**, testament, valuable, vibrant
- **Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o era):** align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant
- **Mid-2025 onward (GPT-5 era):** emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing, plus the notability/coverage vocabulary from 1.2
- **Grok idiosyncrasy:** pseudo-scientific words — *causal, empirical, correlate* — and continued *underscore* into 2026.

Notes: "delve" famously peaked in 2023–24 then dropped sharply. A word being overused does **not** mean its synonyms are — the tell is these exact words. Context matters (a literal underscore character is not a tell). In chat comments (not articles), overuse of **"concrete"** ("no concrete evidence", "concrete examples") is an additional tell.

Illustrative pile-up from one real example: "Somali cuisine is an **intricate** and diverse fusion... drawing from the **rich tapestry** of Arab, Indian, and Italian flavours. This culinary **tapestry** is a direct result of Somalia's longstanding heritage of **vibrant** trade... **Additionally**... cherished and fundamental elements in the rich **tapestry**... An **enduring testament** to the influence of Italian colonial rule... in the local culinary **landscape**, **showcasing** how these dishes have integrated... **Additionally**, Somali merchants played a **pivotal** role..."

#### 2.2 Avoidance of basic copulas ("is"/"are")

**Words to watch:** *serves as/stands as/marks/represents [a], boasts/features/maintains/offers [a], refers to*

AI replaces plain *is/has* with dressed-up constructions. One study found a 10%+ drop in *is/are* frequency in academic writing in 2023, with no prior trend; the same decline appears when GPT is asked to "revise" sentences. Marketing verbs (*features, offers*) replace neutral *has*. Newer output gets more elaborate: "ventured into politics as a candidate" vs. "was a candidate"; "began his career as" vs. "was."

Real before/after from an AI copyedit: "Gallery 825 ... **is** LAAA's exhibition arm" → "Gallery 825 **serves as** LAAA's exhibition space... The gallery **features** four separate spaces."

#### 2.3 Negative parallelisms

Constructions framing a subject against what it is not, as if correcting a misconception nobody holds:

- **"Not only X, but (also) Y":** "This choice of language is not only dismissive but also unnecessarily harsh"; "constitutes not only a work of self-representation, but a visual document of her obsessions"
- **"Not just X — Y":** "This remark doesn't just undermine the editor's argument; it questions their very right to participate"; "That's not just a sourcing issue — it's a systemic bias"
- **"Not X, but Y":** "not grounded in visual mastery, but in..."; "not a mirror but a portal: not a representation of self, but a mechanism for its constant reinvention"; "Not a career, not a body of work, not sustained relevance — just an algorithmic moment"
- **Reversed ("X rather than Y")**, particularly common in Grok output: "prioritizing empirical consolidation of power... rather than ideological purity"
- Multi-sentence versions: "He hailed from the esteemed Duse family... Eugenio's life, **however**, took a path that intertwined both personal ambition and familial complexities."

Humans do use contrast — especially in "myths busted" writing — but AI uses it as constant decoration. Stereotypically the single most recognized "AI sign."

#### 2.4 Rule of three

LLMs overuse triplets in every form: "adjective, adjective, adjective"; "short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase"; three parallel bullet groups. Used to make superficial analysis appear comprehensive: "emphasizing unity, togetherness, and transcendent faith"; "authenticity, consent, and dignity"; "critique, correction, and clarity."

#### 2.5 Forced lexical variety ("elegant variation")

Generative AI uses repetition penalties, so instead of naturally repeating a word it cycles through synonyms mechanically. The result reads like a thesaurus exercise. (Caveat: many non-native English speakers were explicitly taught to avoid repetition — Italian schools, for example — so weigh this with other signs. Also, separate AI generations pasted together may not show it.)

#### 2.6 Didactic disclaimers (mostly 2023–2024)

**Words to watch:** *it's important/critical/crucial to note/remember/consider, worth noting, may vary...*

Lecturing an imagined reader about safety, nuance, or jurisdiction differences nobody asked about: "However, it's important to note that these caucuses operate outside the formal structure and their influence may vary." / "It's important to remember that what's free in one country might not be free in another." Largely a GPT-4-era "partial refusal" reflex, now rarer but not gone.

#### 2.7 Section summaries (mostly older models)

**Words to watch:** *In summary, In conclusion, Overall*

Ending paragraphs, sections, or whole texts by restating what was just said — often under a literal "Conclusion" heading. "In summary, the educational and training trajectory for nurse scientists typically involves..." after a text that said exactly that.

#### 2.8 Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers and speculation about gaps

**Words to watch:** *as of my last knowledge update, up to my last training update, while specific details are limited/scarce..., not widely available/documented/disclosed, ...in the provided/available sources/search results..., based on available information*

Older models disclosed their cutoff: "As of my last knowledge update in January 2022, I don't have specific information about..." Newer search-enabled models produce a subtler version: claiming information "is not widely documented" and then **speculating anyway** — "While specific information about the fauna is limited in the provided search results, the mountain **likely supports**..." The speculation (including the claim that something is "not documented") may be entirely fabricated. When the gap concerns a person's private life, AI claims they "maintain a low profile" or "keep personal details private" — also speculation.

#### 2.9 Prompt refusals and chatbot voice (older)

**Words to watch:** *as an AI language model, as a large language model, I cannot offer medical advice, but I can..., I'm sorry...*

Leftover refusal text pasted into documents: "As an AI language model, I can't directly add content for you, but I can help you draft your bibliography."

### PART 3: STYLE AND FORMATTING

#### 3.1 Title Case in Headings

AI strongly tends to Capitalize All Main Words in section headings ("Impact of Technology and Digitalization", "Sustainable Development and Environmental Law"), where human convention in many contexts is sentence case.

#### 3.2 Overuse of boldface

Mechanical emphasis in a "key takeaways" fashion — often bolding **every instance** of a chosen word or phrase throughout a text. Inherited from readmes, fan wikis, how-tos, sales pitches, slide decks, and listicles. Some newer models are explicitly instructed to tone it down.

#### 3.3 Inline-header vertical lists

The signature AI structure: a bullet or number → **Bolded Header:** → colon → descriptive text.

> **Standard Rotary Saws:** Typically used for drywall and light materials.
> **Heavy-Duty Rotary Saws:** Designed for tougher materials such as tiles, metals, and plastics.

Variants: bullets rendered as •, -, –, #, or emoji; explicit "1." numbering; sometimes no punctuation at all between the mini-header and its text. Whole documents built from these lists instead of prose.

#### 3.4 Em-dash overuse

LLM output uses em dashes (—) more often than comparable non-professional human writing, and in places where humans would use commas, parentheses, or colons. Usage is formulaic — mimicking "punched-up" sales copy, over-emphasizing clauses and parallelisms — and AI em dashes usually have spaces around them, contrary to common typographic style.

Real sample: "When policy says 'one event,' it's shorthand — and the supporting essays show... This isn't 'imagining' what policy should be — it's recognizing how consensus has shaped its application... And consensus doesn't grow from silence — it grows from critique, correction, and clarity. If we disagree on that, then yes — we're speaking different languages." (Four in a short passage, plus a rule of three.)

Weigh this sign **in combination** with others: humans love em dashes too, and newer models (notably GPT-5.1) suppress them because the tell became notorious.

#### 3.5 Emoji as formatting

Emoji decorating section headings or bullet points: 🚀 ✅ 💡 📌 🧭 🧠 — "🌏 My Interests", "🎯 What I'm Working On", "📬 Let's Connect!". Now rarer in chatbot output but still seen.

#### 3.6 Curly quotation marks and apostrophes

ChatGPT and DeepSeek typically output curly quotes (“ ” ‘ ’) and curly apostrophes (’), sometimes inconsistently mixed with straight ones in a single text. **Weak evidence alone:** Word's smart quotes, macOS/iOS defaults, grammar tools, and professional typesetting all produce them; Gemini and Claude typically don't use them at all.

#### 3.7 Unnecessary small tables

Tiny tables for information that reads naturally as a sentence — e.g., a two-row "Key Statistics" table holding a market value and a facilities list.

#### 3.8 Skipped heading levels and thematic breaks

Starting from third-level headings without second-level ones, and inserting horizontal-rule breaks (`---`) before each heading — Markdown habits that look wrong in most publishing contexts.

### PART 4: COPY-PASTE ARTIFACTS

Leftovers that near-definitively reveal chatbot involvement:

- **Chatbot correspondence left in the text:** *"I hope this helps", "Certainly!", "Of course!", "You're absolutely right!", "Would you like me to...", "Is there anything else", "Let me know", "Here is a..."* — plus offers like "Would you like help formatting and submitting this?"
- **Phrasal templates never filled in:** "[Insert your name]", "[Describe the specific section or content that needs editing]", "I am writing to express my deep concern about... the article about [Entertainer's Name]"
- **Placeholder values:** dates like `2025-XX-XX`, URLs like `PASTE_SPOTIFY_TRACK_URL_HERE`, `INSERT_SOURCE_URL_30`, `SOURCE_PUBLISHER`
- **Machine citation artifacts** (each tied to a specific tool):
  - `citeturn0search0`, `turn0image0` — ChatGPT
  - `:contentReference[oaicite:16]{index=16}`, `oai_citation` — ChatGPT bugs
  - `utm_source=chatgpt.com` / `utm_source=openai` in URLs — ChatGPT; `utm_source=copilot.com` — Microsoft Copilot; `referrer=grok.com` — Grok
  - `[cite: 1]`, `[cite: 3, 12, 13]` — Gemini
  - `【85†L261-269】` (lenticular brackets + dagger) — DeepSeek and derivatives
  - `[attached_file:1]`, `[web:1]` — Perplexity
  - `grok-card` / `grok_render_citation_card_json` XML fragments — Grok
  - `({"attribution":{"attributableIndex":"1009-1"}})` JSON — ChatGPT
  - `:::writing{variant="document" id="68427"}` — seen from mid-2026
  - `↩` characters marking footnotes
- **Fabricated references:** links that never existed (no archive copies), DOIs resolving to unrelated papers, book citations with no page numbers or with pages that don't verify the claim, invalid ISBN checksums, authors who were dead at the purported writing date.
- **Mixed markup languages:** Markdown syntax (`**bold**`, `## headings`, ` ``` ` fences) appearing where the platform uses something else — a strong sign the text was pasted from a chatbot interface.

### PART 5: BEHAVIORAL / CONTEXTUAL SIGNS

- **Pronounced shift in writing style:** suddenly flawless formal prose from someone whose other messages read differently — especially if their older writing predates November 2022. The reverse also helps: a consistent style across pre- and post-2022 writing suggests human text.
- **English-variety mismatch:** e.g., an Indian writer covering an Indian topic in flawless American English (LLMs default to American English). Weigh carefully — non-native speakers mix varieties, and people code-switch by venue.
- **Style that tracks model eras:** a long-term AI user's 2023 texts resemble 2023 LLM output, their 2025 texts resemble 2025 output.
- **Overwhelmingly exhaustive summaries/notes:** formal first-person paragraph-length explanations of trivial changes, itemizing what the author "ensured" or "avoided" doing.
- **Inability to explain one's own choices:** a human who inserted a wrong link can usually explain the mix-up; someone who pasted AI output cannot.
- **Timing:** text written before November 30, 2022 (ChatGPT's public launch) can be safely presumed human.
- **Model idiolects:** each model family has a distinct voice — e.g., ChatGPT and Grok focus more on "broader context" than Gemini and Claude; Gemini and Claude tend to be more concise.

---

### PART 6: SIGNS OF HUMAN WRITING

Empirically more common in human text than in AI text — most of it perfectly good style:

- **Simple is/has constructions:** *there is a, it has a*
- **Plain verbs over stiff or euphemistic synonyms:** *wrote* (not authored), *moved* (not relocated), *used* (not utilized), *tried* (not attempted), *died* (not passed away)
- **Superlative or definitive statements:** *one of the best, is the only, was the first* (AI avoids committing)
- **Hedging qualifiers and intensifiers:** *very, perhaps, tends to*
- **Wordy connective constructions:** *as a result of, in order to, all of the, a part of, the fact that*
- Uneven rhythm, digressions, unexpected specificity, and the occasional genuine error.

---

### PART 7: INEFFECTIVE INDICATORS — DON'T RELY ON THESE

False accusations drive people away and poison trust. These commonly cited "tells" are weak or meaningless:

- **Perfect grammar** — many humans are skilled or professionally trained writers.
- **Mixed casual and formal registers, or "clinical yet emotional" tone** — can indicate a technical background, youth, playfulness, neurodivergence, or multiple authors.
- **"Bland" or "robotic" prose** — too subjective; the actual tells are specific and listed above.
- **"Fancy," academic, or formal prose in general** — LLMs overuse *specific* words; the correlation doesn't extend to all formal writing.
- **Letter-like formality alone** — salutations and sign-offs long predate LLMs; some people just write that way.
- **Transition words in isolation** — only a handful (*Additionally, Consequently, Notably*, sentence-initial) are genuinely overrepresented; transitions in general are accepted style.
- **Unsourced content** — the vast majority of unsourced text online predates LLMs; meanwhile, modern chatbots do produce citations (accuracy is another matter).
- **Correct or bizarre technical formatting** — both usually have mundane human or software explanations.
